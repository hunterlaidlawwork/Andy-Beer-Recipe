00About Grandad

Grandad was the kind of bloke who treated brewing like a quiet ritual. Same kettle, same spoon, same stool in the corner of the shed. He never wrote any of it down — he just did it, the same way, every time. The beer was always good.

This guide is what he taught, end to end. Simple process. No shortcuts. No fancy gadgets. The kind of beer you'd be proud to hand a mate.

If you've never brewed before, follow it top to bottom. If you have — read the troubleshooting section. He had an answer for nearly every off-flavour you can chase.

Sanitize. Be patient. Don't be a hero.

01The Golden Rules

① Sanitize Everything

If it touches your beer after the boil, it has to be sanitized. Spoons, hoses, hands, the lid of the bucket. No exceptions.

② Temperature Wins

Most off-flavours come from fermenting too warm. Aim for 18–22°C and don't let it spike. Cool place, dark corner.

③ Be Patient

Don't transfer early. Don't bottle early. Don't drink early. The fridge is not a shortcut — time is the ingredient nobody sells.

Grandad's #1 Rule: If the airlock is still bubbling, it's not done. If you can't tell, leave it another two days. You will never regret waiting.

03Ingredients — The Four Sacred Things

Beer is four things: water, malt, hops, yeast. That's it. Everything else is variation. Grandad used a malt extract base — easy, repeatable, and good every time.

💧 Water

About 90% of your beer. If your tap water tastes good cold, it'll make good beer. If it's heavily chlorinated, fill a clean bucket the day before and let it sit, or boil it for 15 minutes. Filtered is fine. Distilled is too soft.

Avoid: Softened water (the salt kills yeast), or anything you wouldn't drink straight.

🌾 Malt Extract

Comes as a tin of liquid malt extract (LME) or a bag of dry malt extract (DME). Grandad bought the kit cans — pre-hopped, ready to go. Pop the lid, sit the tin in hot water to loosen, pour it in.

Common kits: Coopers, Mangrove Jack's, Black Rock — all sold at Brew For Less.

🍯 Dextrose / Brewing Sugar

The fuel. ~1 kg per 23 L batch is the standard. You can substitute up to 500 g with light dry malt for more body and a fuller flavour.

04Sanitizing — Don't Skip This

Sanitizing is the single biggest difference between good homebrew and the kind that tastes like wet cardboard. Grandad was militant about it.

How to Sanitize Properly

  1. Clean first. Sanitizer is not a cleaner — it doesn't remove gunk. Wash everything with PBW or unscented dishwashing detergent first. Rinse well.
  2. Mix sanitizer fresh. No-rinse like StarSan: 1.5 ml per 1 L of water. Mix in your fermenter and slosh it around to coat every surface.
  3. Coat everything. Hoses, taps, spoon, thermometer, lid, airlock, anything that will touch beer.
  4. Don't fear the foam. StarSan foams up. The foam is your friend — it stays sanitized. "Don't fear the foam" was a Grandad line.
  5. Drip dry. Don't wipe with a towel. Don't rinse with tap water. Just drain and use.
Never use: Bleach (residue ruins beer), scented detergents (taint), or wipes with fragrance. Stick to brewing-grade sanitizer.

05Brew Day — Step by Step

Set aside about 90 minutes. Put music on. Don't rush. The whole thing is a 6-step rhythm.

Brew Day: kettle → fermenter → airlock

Step 1 — Sanitize

Mix your no-rinse sanitizer in the fermenter. Slosh it around. Tip it through the tap into a bucket and use that to dunk your spoon, hydrometer, lid, and airlock. Drain the fermenter — don't dry it.

Step 2 — Heat the Tin

Sit the malt extract tin (and yeast sachet, if it's under the lid) in 5 cm of hot tap water for 5 minutes. This makes the malt pour cleanly without sticking to the tin.

If you're using DME instead, just measure it out — no heating needed.

Step 3 — Mix the Wort

  1. Add 2 L of boiling water to the empty (sanitized) fermenter.
  2. Pour in the malt extract. Rinse the tin with hot water and add that too — don't waste any.
  3. Add 1 kg of dextrose (or your brew enhancer).
  4. Stir well with the long-handled spoon until everything is dissolved.
  5. Top up with cold water to 23 L.

Step 4 — Cool & Check Temp

Stir to mix and check the temperature. You want it under 25°C — ideally between 18–22°C. If it's still warm, sit the fermenter in a sink of cold water or wait. Pitching warm = stressed yeast = off-flavours.

Step 5 — Take an OG Reading

Draw a sample with the sanitized hydrometer trial jar. Read the gravity at the bottom of the meniscus. Write it down. Typical OG: 1.040–1.050.

Drink the sample if you like — it's just sweet, flat wort. Don't pour it back.

Step 6 — Pitch the Yeast

Sprinkle the yeast over the surface. Don't stir — it'll find its way down. Put the lid on, fit the bung and airlock (with sanitizer/water in it), and move it somewhere dark and stable around 18–22°C.

Walk away. Don't open the lid for at least 7 days. Don't peek. Don't shake it.

06Fermenting — The Quiet Phase

What's Happening

The yeast is eating the sugars and producing alcohol and CO₂. The CO₂ pushes out through the airlock — you'll hear it bubble for the first 3–7 days. Then it slows down. Then it stops. That's normal.

What to Watch

  • Temperature: 18–22°C, steady. Spikes cause fusels (harsh, hot-tasting).
  • Airlock activity: Active in days 1–4, slowing days 5–7, still by day 8–10.
  • The krausen: A foamy head on top of the beer. Normal. Will collapse back down.
  • Smell: Bready, fruity, sometimes a bit sulphurous early on. All fine.
How do you know it's done? Take a hydrometer reading. Wait 2 days. Take another. If the number hasn't moved, it's done. Don't trust the airlock alone — if the seal isn't perfect, it'll never bubble even if fermentation is raging.

Optional: Dry Hopping

For more aroma, drop in 30–50 g of hop pellets in the last 4–5 days of fermentation. Don't worry about contamination — the alcohol and CO₂ blanket protect the beer. Just sanitize the bag or muslin you're using.

07Bottling — Where Most Brews Are Lost

This is the bit where rushing or sloppy sanitation kills good beer. Take an extra hour. Get it right.

Bottling: fermenter → priming → bottles → caps

What You Need Ready

  • 50 sanitized 500 ml bottles (or 24× 750 ml)
  • Fresh, sanitized crown caps
  • Bottle capper
  • Sanitized siphon and bottling wand
  • A second sanitized bucket (the "bottling bucket") with a tap
  • 140 g dextrose dissolved in 250 ml boiled water — cooled

The Process

  1. Final hydrometer reading. Confirm fermentation is finished. Write down the FG (typically 1.008–1.012).
  2. Prime. Pour the dissolved priming sugar into the empty bottling bucket.
  3. Siphon. Gently siphon the beer from the fermenter into the bottling bucket. The flow stirs the priming sugar evenly. Do not splash. The end of the hose stays under the surface.
  4. Leave the trub. The sludge at the bottom of the fermenter stays put. Stop the siphon before it pulls in any.
  5. Fill bottles. Open the tap, push the bottling wand to the bottom of the bottle, fill until it overflows the wand, lift to stop. The space the wand displaces becomes your headspace — perfect every time.
  6. Cap. Drop a sanitized cap on top, set the capper, push down hard. Listen for the click.
  7. Date them. Write the bottle date on the cap or a sticker. You'll thank yourself later.
Grandad's rule: If the airlock has been still for 48 hours and two hydrometer readings agree — only then bottle. Bottling early = bottle bombs. Glass shrapnel in your shed is not a homebrew flex.

08Conditioning — The Wait

  • Store the bottles upright at 18–22°C, dark.
  • Wait at least 2 weeks. 3 weeks is better. 4 weeks is great.
  • After 2 weeks, chill one bottle for 24 hours and try it. If it pours with a head, it's carbed.
  • Once carbed, you can move them all to a cool spot or fridge for long-term storage.
  • Most ales improve up to ~3 months in the bottle. After that, slowly downhill.

09The Full Timeline

Day 0 — Brew Day

Sanitize, mix wort, pitch yeast, seal up. ~90 minutes.

Day 1–4 — Active Fermentation

Airlock bubbling, foam on top, temperature steady. Don't open it.

Day 5–9 — Slowing Down

Bubbles stretch out. Krausen falls. Yeast settles.

Day 10–14 — Confirm Done

Two hydrometer readings 48 hours apart, identical. Optional dry hop in this window.

Day 14 (approx) — Bottling Day

Prime, siphon, bottle, cap, date.

Day 14–28 — Conditioning

Bottles at room temperature. Carbonation builds.

Day 28+ — Drink

Chill, pour carefully (sediment stays in the bottle), enjoy.

11Troubleshooting

Grandad's diagnostics, in order of "did you check the obvious thing first?"

SymptomLikely CauseFix
Airlock not bubbling after 48 h Bad lid seal, or fermenting silently Take a hydrometer reading. If gravity is dropping, it's working. Re-seat the lid.
Cidery / sweet apple taste Too much corn sugar, fermented too warm Replace 250 g dextrose with DME next batch. Keep ferment at 19°C.
Sour or vinegar smell Acetobacter — oxygen got in Tip it. Re-sanitize everything. Don't splash during transfers.
Wet cardboard / sherry taste Oxidation Less splashing. Cap fast. Drink younger.
Banana / clove flavours Wheat / Belgian yeast, or fermenting too warm Drop ferment temp to 18°C with cooler/heat belt.
Flat beer after 3 weeks Not enough priming sugar, or weak yeast at bottling Wait another week. If still flat, the yeast is exhausted — drink them as still beer or pitch fresh.
Gushers (foam everywhere) Bottled too early, or contamination Chill all bottles immediately to slow it. Drink fast. Next batch: confirm FG is stable.
Bottle bombs Bottled before fermentation finished Move all bottles into a sturdy box, outdoors if possible. Always FG-check before bottling.
Cloudy beer Yeast in suspension, or chill haze Cold-condition in the fridge for a week. Pour gently, sediment stays in bottle.
Off / medicinal flavour Chlorine in tap water Use filtered water, or boil and cool tap water the night before.

12Glossary

ABV

Alcohol By Volume. Roughly (OG − FG) × 131.

Airlock

The little water-filled valve on top of the fermenter. Lets CO₂ out, keeps everything else out.

Carboy

A glass or plastic jug used as a fermenter, often for secondary or longer-conditioning beers.

Dextrose

Brewing sugar. Pure glucose. Ferments cleanly with no flavour contribution.

FG (Final Gravity)

Hydrometer reading after fermentation finishes. Typically 1.008–1.014.

Hydrometer

Floating glass instrument that measures sugar in liquid. Tells you when fermentation is done.

Krausen

Foamy yeast head on top of fermenting beer. Looks dramatic. Totally normal.

LME / DME

Liquid Malt Extract / Dry Malt Extract. The malted barley sugar base of your beer.

OG (Original Gravity)

Hydrometer reading before fermentation. Typically 1.040–1.055.

Pitch

Adding the yeast to the wort.

Priming Sugar

Small dose of sugar added at bottling so yeast can carbonate the beer in the bottle.

Trub

The sludge of dead yeast and protein at the bottom of the fermenter. Leave it behind when bottling.

Wort

Unfermented beer. Sweet, sticky, smells like malt. Wort + yeast + time = beer.

Dry Hop

Adding hops to fermenting beer (not the boil) for aroma without bitterness.

13FAQ

Do I really need a hydrometer?

Yes. Without one, you're guessing. It's a $15 piece of glass that prevents bottle bombs and tells you exactly when fermentation is done. Grandad was emphatic on this.

How long does homebrew last?

Bottle-conditioned ales typically peak between 3 weeks and 3 months. Stouts and stronger beers improve longer — 6+ months. Lighter pales fade after 2 months.

Can I reuse bottles from the shop?

Yes — anything with a crown cap (not screw-tops). Rinse straight after drinking, store dry. Sanitize before bottling day. Save labels for an evening with hot water.

What's the cheapest way to start?

A starter kit from Brew For Less includes the fermenter, airlock, hydrometer, sanitizer, capper and a beer kit. Add bottles (or save 50 from your fridge) and you're brewing.

Is homebrew legal?

In Australia, yes — up to a generous personal limit, no licence required. Don't sell it. Check your local rules if you're elsewhere.

Why does my brew smell like rotten eggs in week one?

Sulphur compounds from active fermentation. They blow off through the airlock as fermentation progresses. By bottling day, gone. Don't panic.

Can I open the lid to check?

Once, briefly, after day 7 to take a hydrometer reading — fine. Constantly opening it lets oxygen in and risks contamination. Trust the process.

What if I don't have a temperature-controlled space?

Find your house's most stable spot — usually a cupboard or under a bench. A wet T-shirt over the fermenter with a fan beside it cools by ~3°C through evaporation. In winter, a heat belt costs about $25 at Brew For Less.

What's Grandad's most important lesson?

"Sanitize. Be patient. Don't be a hero." Three rules. That's the whole bible.

14Grandad's Hard-Won Tips

Buy in bulk.

Bottles, caps, sanitizer and yeast packets keep forever. You'll always run out at the worst moment.

Number your batches.

Marker on the cap. Write the recipe + date in a notebook. After 5 brews you'll forget which was which.

Save the best bottle.

Tuck one away for 6 months. Compare it to a fresh one. You'll learn what time does to your beer.

Don't invest before you've brewed three.

Fancy gadgets aren't needed until you know what's actually limiting your beer.

Pour at 45°.

Down the side of the glass. Last 1 cm stays in the bottle. That's where the yeast sediment is.

Share it.

The beer is better when someone you like is drinking it with you. Grandad never brewed alone.
